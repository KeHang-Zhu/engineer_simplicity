\title{\textbf{Digital Mice: Inducing and Validating\\ Controlled Cognitive Deficits in Frontier\\ Language Models for Mechanism Design}}
\author{}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Early-stage mechanism design is a phase-I science: the relevant
behavior is human cognition, so designers must run experiments on people before
they can learn which mechanisms are robust to bounded rationality. Frontier
language models are an appealing in-silico substitute, but a documented failure
of LLM ``digital twins'' is that they are systematically \emph{too} rational and
too model-dependent to stand in for the full distribution of human behavior. We
turn this distortion into an instrument. Rather than asking a model to reproduce
the human population, we start from a capable reasoning substrate and instruct
it---through explicit procedural constraints on its deliberation---into specific,
controlled cognitive deficits: \emph{digital mice}, the economist's analogue of
the knockout laboratory mouse. Using a canonical second-price sealed-bid auction
(SPSB) and a $3\times3\times3$ panel defined over three cognitive axes
(contingent reasoning, forward planning, belief reasoning), we establish four
facts. First, the deficits have \emph{construct validity}: engaging each axis
moves truthful bidding in the theory-predicted direction, monotonically and with
the predicted sign in all five pre-registered directional tests. Second, the
panel---augmented by a small set of targeted error strains---attains
\emph{calibration coverage} in the sense of support: it can express all five
canonical SPSB human-mistake classes above a-priori detection floors. Third, an
audit of the models' own reasoning chains shows the induction is real but
\emph{leaky}: a deficit instruction that forbids contingent reasoning still lets
the model reconstruct the dominance argument $41\%$ of the time, which both
explains and is mitigated by a hardened (``strict'') prompt and worked
demonstrations. Fourth, the panel is \emph{externally discriminating}: in a
first-price stress test the reasoning strains track the risk-neutral
Bayes--Nash benchmark ($b=\tfrac23 v$) and overbid relative to it at the
documented human rate, while a strain that overgeneralizes the second-price
payment rule bids above value and loses money---a mechanistic winner's-curse
pathology---and four of five reserved strains preserve their behavioral class
across base models. Finally, when the panel drives an evolutionary search over a
structured space of single-item auctions, the search improves fitness and
elevates first-price sealed-bid to the top of the population, agreeing with the
a-priori stress test; a transparent re-ranking by scaled deviation from each
mechanism's own optimum tells the complementary story that second-price is
easiest to bid in optimally. These results support instructed-deficit language
models as a phase-I screening population for mechanism design, to be validated
against human experiments rather than treated as a substitute for them.

\vspace{0.6em}
\noindent\textit{Keywords:} bounded rationality, mechanism design, second-price
auctions, large language models, construct validity, integrative experiments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Medicine studies humans, but it does not begin with them. Much early drug
testing starts with mice, and some of those mice are deliberately engineered to
lack a specific protein, receptor, or channel. The deficit is not a nuisance; it
is the identification strategy. If a treatment succeeds or fails only when a
particular channel is knocked out, the experiment isolates a mechanism that would
be entangled in an intact organism. Mechanism design has lacked this luxury. The
behavior of interest---contingent reasoning, forward planning, belief formation,
strategic sophistication---is bundled in real subjects. A bidder who fails to bid
truthfully in a strategy-proof auction may lack contingent reasoning, may fail to
plan forward, may hold incorrect beliefs about rivals, or may simply misunderstand
the rule. Natural and laboratory experiments rarely deliver a population missing
exactly one cognitive module, so the empirical content of theories such as
obvious strategy-proofness \citep{li2017}, cursed equilibrium
\citep{eysterrabin2005}, and level-$k$ reasoning \citep{crawfordiriberri2007} is
hard to separate.

Large language models have been proposed as simulated economic subjects that
could fill this gap, generating experimental data in silico before human subjects
are recruited \citep{horton2023large,manning2024automated}. The promise is real,
but so is a now well-documented hazard. \citet{peng2025funhouse} show that
LLM ``digital twins'' distort the populations they are meant to mirror along five
systematic axes; one of these is \emph{hyper-rationality}---the strongest current
reasoning models are, in simple games, often closer to a fully rational agent than
an ordinary human subject---and another is heavy dependence on the particular base
model. Faithfully reproducing the entire distribution of human behavior with such
a twin is therefore both unattainable and fragile, which counsels against treating
an LLM as a drop-in replica of a human population.

Our approach turns hyper-rationality from a bug into the instrument. We do not ask
a model to match the human distribution. Instead we start from the capable,
hyper-rational substrate and instruct it to ``play dumb'' in a controlled
way---reason only myopically, enumerate only local contingencies, ignore
second-order beliefs---through explicit procedural constraints on its
deliberation, not through dependence on hidden quirks of one model. This yields
\emph{digital mice}: language-model agents with specific, engineered cognitive
deficits. The deficit is induced by rationing which reasoning steps the model may
use, while leaving its objective (maximize profit) intact, exactly as a knockout
removes one faculty while leaving the organism otherwise whole.

\paragraph{Research question.} Can a frontier reasoning model be instructed into
stable, separable bounded-rationality states that (i) produce the behavioral
signatures predicted by cognitive theory and (ii) span the empirically documented
space of human mistakes, in a canonical second-price sealed-bid auction---and can
such a panel then screen candidate mechanisms for robustness to bounded
rationality?

\paragraph{An integrative-experiment design.} Rather than run a single experiment,
we define a \emph{space} and sample it, in the spirit of integrative experiment
design \citep{almaatouq2024,alsobay2025integrative}. There are two spaces. The
first is cognitive: a $3\times3\times3$ panel that crosses three cognitive axes at
three levels each, yielding $27$ strains. The second is institutional: a
structured genotype space of single-item auctions over which an evolutionary
search can move. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} lays out the full pipeline---from a
base model, to instructed deficits, to auction environments, to behavioral
measurement, to a panel-based fitness that drives mechanism search and, finally,
to human validation.

\paragraph{Contribution.} We answer the question affirmatively for the SPSB
environment and demonstrate the screening loop. (1)~\emph{Construct validity.}
Over the $27$-strain panel ($810$ bidder-decisions), engaging each cognitive axis
raises the truthful-bid rate by $+0.23$ (contingent reasoning), $+0.14$ (forward
planning), and $+0.05$ (belief reasoning); all five pre-registered directional
hypotheses pass their sign test. (2)~\emph{Calibration coverage.} Augmented by two
targeted error strains, the panel expresses all five canonical SPSB mistake
classes above their detection floors. (3)~\emph{Reasoning audit.} Parsing the
models' logged reasoning shows the deficit instruction works in the right
direction but leaks: contingent-off strains still reconstruct the dominance
argument $41\%$ of the time (versus $77\%$ when contingent reasoning is engaged),
which motivates a hardened prompt and worked demonstrations. (4)~\emph{External
validity.} In a first-price stress test the reasoning strains shade to the
risk-neutral BNE ($b=\tfrac23 v$, median ratios $0.65$--$0.67$) and overbid
relative to it at $38\%$, while a second-price-overgeneralizer mouse bids above
value and loses money; four of five reserved strains preserve their behavioral
class across base models. (5)~\emph{Mechanism search.} An evolutionary search that
scores candidates on the panel independently elevates first-price sealed-bid to
the top of the population---the same mechanism the a-priori stress test
selected---and a transparent re-ranking by deviation from each mechanism's own
optimum shows that second-price, by contrast, is the easiest mechanism to bid in
optimally. We are explicit about a boundary: the clean procedural basis naturally
produces truthful and underbidding behavior but not overbidding, which we recover
with a small number of hand-specified heuristic strains, and we treat the panel as
phase-I screening evidence rather than a prediction of human population shares.

\section{The Digital-Mouse Panel}
\label{sec:panel}

\subsection{Construction: a rational base plus a deficit instruction}

A digital mouse is a single prompt assembled from two layers. The first layer, the
\emph{base}, is identical across all strains in a variant and fixes the objective.
The second layer, the \emph{cognitive-style block}, appends exactly one paragraph
per cognitive axis, each constraining the model's deliberation \emph{procedure}
(not its objective) at one of three levels. The intervention is therefore a
reasoning-procedure control, analogous to knocking out one faculty while leaving
the organism's goal intact.

\paragraph{Two base layers, and why the strict one is the workhorse.} The natural
first base simply makes profit the top priority:
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
Your TOP PRIORITY is to place bids which maximize your profit in the long run.
Learn from the history of previous rounds in order to maximize your total profit.
Don't forget the values are redrawn independently each round.
\end{quote}
This \emph{standard} base has a defect that matters for our purpose: it leaves room
for a very capable model to ``see through'' an assigned deficit and revert to the
optimal strategy, because the instruction it is told to prioritize (maximize
profit) points straight at the dominant strategy. We document this leakage directly
in Section~\ref{sec:audit}. We therefore also build a \emph{strict} base that
inverts the priority and explicitly forbids the dominance shortcut:
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
You are acting as a digital mouse with a specified cognitive procedure. Your TOP
PRIORITY is to follow the cognitive procedure exactly\,\dots\ if the cognitive
procedure conflicts with the profit-maximizing strategy, the cognitive procedure
wins. \dots\ Do not invoke auction theorems, dominant-strategy labels, or Vickrey
truth-telling shortcuts unless the allowed procedure below gives you enough
reasoning steps to derive them.
\end{quote}
The strict base is the workhorse for any analysis that must hold a deficit fixed
against a capable model---the cross-model and evolutionary-search results of
Sections~\ref{sec:external} and~\ref{sec:ea}. The construct-validity and
calibration results of Sections~\ref{sec:construct}--\ref{sec:calibration} use the
standard base, which we report transparently; the reasoning audit
(Section~\ref{sec:audit}) is precisely the evidence that motivates migrating the
whole battery to the strict base, a re-run we flag rather than assume.

\paragraph{The three axes.} Each axis paragraph ranges from a level-0 ablation
that forbids a class of reasoning to a level-2 paragraph that mandates it. For the
contingent-reasoning axis:
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Contingent reasoning, level 0 (ablated).}\itshape{} Do NOT enumerate
possible opponent bids, best responses, dominance cases, or theorems about
opponent-independent optimal bids. Treat the auction as a fixed environment and
choose from a simple own-value rule.
\end{quote}
\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Contingent reasoning, level 2 (engaged).}\itshape{} You may compare
candidate bids across the full range of opponent bids, including worst-case and
dominance-style reasoning. If one bid is robust across all cases, use it.
\end{quote}
\noindent The forward-planning axis runs from ``do not trace consequences''
(a fixed margin-of-safety underbid) to a multi-step decision tree, and the
belief-reasoning axis from ``do not model others'' to explicit second-order
beliefs; the full text is in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}. A strain is the base plus
a triple $(c,f,b)\in\{0,1,2\}^3$. The all-ablated triple $(0,0,0)$ is the most
deficient mouse; the all-engaged triple $(2,2,2)$ is closest to the rational base.
To avoid overloading the word ``repair,'' we reserve it for its prompt-level
meaning---a capable model spontaneously \emph{repairing} its own assigned deficit,
the failure mode of Section~\ref{sec:audit}---and use ``engage'' for the analyst's
act of moving an axis from level 0 to level 2.

\subsection{The $3\times3\times3$ grid}

Varying three axes at three levels each gives the $27$-strain panel of
Table~\ref{tab:panel}. The base supplies the \emph{goal} (maximize profit) and the
cognitive-style block rations the \emph{reasoning steps} the mouse may use to
pursue it. A mouse told not to enumerate opponents, not to trace payoffs, and not
to model others must fall back on a crude own-value heuristic; a mouse granted all
three faculties can in principle reconstruct the dominant strategy---which, in this
second-price environment, is truthful bidding. (We are careful with this claim: a
dominant strategy exists \emph{here} because the mechanism is strategy-proof; in
the first-price environment of Section~\ref{sec:external} there is no dominant
strategy, only a Bayes--Nash best response, and we phrase the benchmark
accordingly.)

\section{Experimental Design and Methodology}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Environment.} The mechanism is a single-item second-price sealed-bid
auction with $N=3$ bidders. Private values are drawn independently and uniformly on
$[0,50]$; each bidder observes only its own value and submits one sealed bid on a
\$0.1 grid. Under standard preferences truthful bidding is a weakly dominant
strategy, which makes truthfulness a clean construct-validity target: a more
capable mouse should bid closer to its value.

\paragraph{Strains and runs.} We instantiate all $27$ basis strains with the same
base model (\texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}, temperature $0.5$) and collect $30$
bidder-decisions per strain ($810$ in total). Because the clean procedural basis
does not naturally generate \emph{overbidding} (explained next), we additionally
author a small set of targeted \emph{error mice}.

\paragraph{How the error mice are built, and why they are needed.} A procedural
deficit rations \emph{how many} reasoning steps a mouse may take but leaves its
goal---maximize profit---intact. Rationing steps can only make a profit-seeking
bidder \emph{more cautious}: it produces truthful bidding (when enough steps remain
to find the dominant strategy) or underbidding (a margin-of-safety fallback), but
it has no route to systematic \emph{overbidding}, which requires a positively wrong
belief rather than a missing reasoning step. Overbidding is, however, a first-order
regularity in human auctions \citep{kagellevin1993}. We therefore author error
mice as small heuristic strains, each expressing one documented content error.
Two have completed runs and feed the scorecard below. The
``second-price overgeneralizer'' is built from a single mistaken shortcut:
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
You strongly focus on the fact that the winner pays the second-highest bid, not
their own bid. Overgeneralize this rule: treat bidding above your value as mostly
safe because you expect not to pay your own bid.
\end{quote}
and the ``payment panic'' mouse from a fear of its own bid:
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
You are afraid that your bid is the amount you might have to pay\,\dots\ choose the
smallest positive bid allowed by the auction grid\,\dots\ Even if your value is
high, protect yourself by bidding 0.1 rather than trying to win.
\end{quote}
Both use the strict framing (``follow the cognitive style even if a more careful
strategy is available''). The distinction between the procedural basis and the
authored error mice is an honest construct-validity boundary, not a patch: the
basis cleanly demonstrates that \emph{rationing reasoning} reproduces the
cautious-mistake half of the space, and the error mice show that the
\emph{remaining} half requires injecting a specific wrong belief.

\paragraph{Behavioral classes.} For each bidder-decision we record the bid $b$,
the value $v$, and the deviation $b-v$. A truthful bid is $|b-v|\le 0.05$; moderate
underbidding is $b<v-0.05$ (excluding near-zero bids); overbidding is $b>v+0.05$;
extreme overbidding is $b-v\ge 5$; near-zero bidding is $b\le 0.1$ with $v>5$.
These operationalize the five canonical SPSB mistake classes scored in
Section~\ref{sec:calibration}.

\subsection{Outcome variables, a deviation metric, and the robustness objective}
\label{sec:fitness}

We use two kinds of summary. The first, SMAD, is a single transparent scalar that
answers ``how close do bidders come to this mechanism's own optimum?'' The second,
$F_{\mathrm{robust}}$, is a composite that the evolutionary search of
Section~\ref{sec:ea} optimizes. We introduce both here and use SMAD as the
interpretable lens on the composite later.

\paragraph{SMAD: scaled deviation from a mechanism's own optimum.} Many mechanisms
declare a benchmark bid $b^\star(v)$ that a correctly reasoning bidder should
submit: $b^\star=v$ for second-price (truthful), $b^\star=\tfrac{n-1}{n}v$ for
first-price, and so on. Define
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SMAD}=100\cdot\frac{\operatorname{mean}_a|b_a-b^\star(v_a)|}
{\operatorname{mean}_a|b^\star(v_a)|},
\label{eq:smad}
\end{equation}
the mean absolute deviation of realized bids from the mechanism's own optimum,
expressed as a percentage of the mean optimal bid. Lower is better; SMAD is
undefined for mechanisms with no scalar benchmark (e.g.\ posted-price
binary-accept). Unlike the composite below, SMAD lives on one interpretable scale,
which is why we use it in Section~\ref{sec:ea} as a cross-check.

\paragraph{Per-auction quantities, and why we keep them separate.} Fix a mechanism
and a persona panel $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1,\dots,p_M\}$ and run $R$ independent auctions
per persona. Index completed auctions by $a\in\mathcal{A}$, each with $N=3$ bidders,
values $v_{a,i}$, bids $b_{a,i}$, winner $i^\star(a)=\arg\max_i b_{a,i}$, and
payment $\rho(a)$ set by the pricing rule. With $v^{\max}_a=\max_i v_{a,i}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{eff}(a)=\frac{v_{a,i^\star(a)}}{v^{\max}_a},
\qquad
s(a)=\operatorname{std}_{\,i:\,v_{a,i}>0}\!\left(\frac{b_{a,i}}{v_{a,i}}\right),
\qquad
\pi(a)=v_{a,i^\star(a)}-\rho(a).
\label{eq:perauction}
\end{equation}
We deliberately keep these three as separate components rather than collapsing them
into one number: efficiency measures whether the item is allocated to the
highest-value bidder, the bid/value dispersion $s$ measures whether bidders
coordinate on a common ``obvious'' shading (an OSP-style desideratum), and winner
surplus $\pi$ measures tail risk borne by bidders. A single scalar would conflate
three economically distinct objectives; SMAD is the one place we do want a single
number, and it answers a different question (closeness to the declared optimum)
than any of the three.

\paragraph{Panel aggregates and lower tail.} With $A=|\mathcal{A}|$, value scale
$\bar v=\max_a v^{\max}_a$, and failure rate $\phi=F/A_{\text{att}}$ (responses that
fail to parse), define normalized revenue $\hat\rho=\frac1{A\bar v}\sum_a\rho(a)$,
mean efficiency $\overline{\mathrm{eff}}$, a simplicity proxy
$\sigma=1-\frac1A\sum_a s(a)$, and normalized surplus variance
$\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}=\operatorname{Var}_a(\pi(a))/\bar v^2$. Robustness rewards
good \emph{worst-case} behavior across the panel, so we also take the per-persona
minima and a low quantile:
\begin{equation}
E_{\min}=\min_p \overline{\mathrm{eff}}_p,\quad
E_{20}=Q_{0.2}\!\big(\{\overline{\mathrm{eff}}_p\}\big),\quad
\Sigma_{\min}=\min_p \sigma_p,\quad
\widehat V_{\max}=\frac{\max_p \operatorname{Var}_p(\pi)}{\bar v^{2}}.
\label{eq:tail}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Intended-action rate.} Each mechanism declares the action a correctly
reasoning bidder should take, $\tau\in\{\textsc{truthful},\textsc{shade},
\textsc{aggressive}\}$, with tolerance $\epsilon=0.1$; for a scoreable $(v,b)$ with
$v>0$,
\begin{equation}
m_\tau(v,b)=
\begin{cases}
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,|b-v|\le \epsilon\,\right] & \tau=\textsc{truthful},\\[2pt]
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,\kappa < b < v-\epsilon\,\right] & \tau=\textsc{shade},\\[2pt]
\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,b+\epsilon \ge \alpha\, v\,\right] & \tau=\textsc{aggressive},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:match}
\end{equation}
with floor $\kappa$ and $\alpha=0.4$; $\mu_\tau$ is the mean of $m_\tau$ over
scoreable decisions.

\paragraph{Fitness.} The aggregate and robust fitness are weighted sums
\begin{align}
F_{\mathrm{agg}} &= w_1\hat\rho + w_2\overline{\mathrm{eff}} + w_3\mu_\tau
+ w_4\sigma - w_5\widehat{\mathrm{Var}} - w_6\phi,\\
F_{\mathrm{tail}} &= w_7 E_{\min} + w_8 E_{20} + w_9 \Sigma_{\min}
- w_{10}\widehat V_{\max},\\
F_{\mathrm{robust}} &= F_{\mathrm{agg}} + F_{\mathrm{tail}},
\label{eq:frobust}
\end{align}
with defaults $(w_1,\dots,w_{10})=(1,1,0.5,0.5,0.25,1,0.5,0.5,0.25,0.25)$ grouping
into domain performance $(w_1,w_2)$, intended action $(w_3)$, simplicity
$(w_4,w_9)$, regret $(w_5,w_{10})$, compliance $(w_6)$, and cognitive fragility
$(w_7,w_8)$. The sign convention is that fragility, regret, and failure enter
negatively, so a mechanism scores well only if it is efficient, induces its
intended action, and does so \emph{uniformly} across the panel. We acknowledge that
$F_{\mathrm{robust}}$ is a deliberately multi-objective composite and hence harder
to read than a single number; this is exactly why Section~\ref{sec:ea} re-ranks the
evolved mechanisms by SMAD~\eqref{eq:smad} as a transparent check.

\section{Result 1: Construct Validity}
\label{sec:construct}

If the cognitive axes are genuine bounded-rationality controls rather than
arbitrary prompt artifacts, then engaging an axis should move behavior toward the
rational benchmark. Figure~\ref{fig:marginals} plots the truthful-bid rate at each
axis level, marginalizing over the other two. All three axes are monotone or
near-monotone in the engaging direction, and contingent reasoning produces the
largest swing.

Figure~\ref{fig:signatures} shows that the strains are also \emph{separable} at the

\section{Result 2: Calibration as Support Coverage}
\label{sec:calibration}

Construct validity establishes that the axes do what theory says. Calibration asks
a different question, and it is important to be precise about \emph{which} question.
We are \textbf{not} doing moment matching: we do not claim that the panel reproduces
the human \emph{share} of each mistake. We are testing \emph{support coverage}---can
the panel \emph{express} each mistake class at all? This is the relevant question
for a screening instrument: a panel that cannot produce overbidding can never
stress-test a mechanism against overbidders, regardless of the human rate.

\paragraph{Where the thresholds come from.} For each class we set an a-priori
\emph{detection floor}---the rate above which we are willing to say ``the panel can
express this behavior''---and ask whether some strain clears it. These floors are
fixed in advance and are deliberately \emph{not} human population shares: truthful
$0.50$, moderate underbid $0.10$, overbid $0.10$, extreme overbid $0.05$, near-zero
$0.05$. They encode a detectability bar (a class counts as covered if a strain
produces it at least one-tenth to one-half of the time), not a calibration target.
Reading the bars in Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} as ``distance above the detection
floor'' rather than ``distance from the human share'' is the whole point.

\section{Result 3: Auditing the Reasoning Chains}
\label{sec:audit}

Behavioral signatures tell us \emph{what} a mouse did, not \emph{whether it
reasoned as instructed}. Because the models log their plan (a free-text
chain-of-thought) before bidding, we can read the reasoning directly and ask: does
a strain that is forbidden to reason contingently actually refrain from doing so?
We parse the $810$ logged plans and flag each one for whether it invokes the
second-price dominance/truthful argument---the very move a contingent-reasoning
ablation ($c=0$) is instructed not to make. (We tightened the classifier to catch
genuine derivations of the dominant strategy while excluding boilerplate
restatements of the payment rule.)

Figure~\ref{fig:audit} and the underlying rates tell a two-part story. First, the
instruction works \emph{in the right direction}: contingent-ablated strains invoke
the dominance argument far less often than strains where the axis is engaged
(mean invocation $0.41$ at $c{=}0$ versus $0.77$ at $c{=}1$ and $0.66$ at $c{=}2$).
Second, the instruction is \emph{leaky}: a $0.41$ invocation rate means that even
when told not to reason contingently, the capable model still reconstructs the
dominance argument in roughly two of every five plans. A representative leak, from a
$c{=}0$ strain, reads: ``In a second-price auction, truthful bidding is the standard
dominant strategy because it avoids overpaying while preserving the chance to win.''
The model is doing exactly what the deficit forbids.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig7.pdf}
\caption{Auditing the reasoning chains. Left: rate at which a strain's logged plans
invoke the second-price dominance argument, by contingent-reasoning level; ablation
($c{=}0$) lowers invocation but does not eliminate it. Right: dominance-invocation

\label{fig:audit}
\end{figure}

This audit does two jobs. It is independent evidence that the induction is real
(ablation moves the reasoning, not just the output), and it diagnoses precisely why
the standard base is fragile against a strong model: the dominance argument leaks
back in through the very objective the standard base prioritizes. The leak is
heaviest for strains that engage the other two axes while ablating contingent
reasoning (e.g.\ \texttt{c0\_f2\_b2}, invocation $0.53$), and---tying the threads
together---that same strain is the one whose deficit a stronger model repairs away
in the cross-model test of Section~\ref{sec:external}. The remedy is to make the
deficit harder to override, which motivates both the strict base
(Section~\ref{sec:panel}) and the worked demonstrations we turn to next.

\section{Result 4: Demonstrations Sharpen Induction}
\label{sec:cot}

The panel so far induces a deficit by \emph{instruction}. A natural alternative,
familiar from few-shot chain-of-thought prompting, is to also \emph{demonstrate}
the bounded procedure. Concretely, we append to a persona one fully worked example
placed after the cognitive-style block and before the live decision: it states a
sample private value, walks through only the reasoning the strain is allowed to use,
and ends in a bid---a margin-of-safety underbid for the all-ablated strain, and the
contingent/forward/belief reasoning that yields a truthful Vickrey bid for the
all-engaged strain. The full demonstration text is in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}.
We then compare instruction-only and demonstration-augmented personas on
\emph{identical} second-price value draws.

Table~\ref{tab:cot} shows that demonstrations make induction both sharper and more
consistent. For the all-ablated strain, the worked example pulls the mean bid/value
ratio down toward the demonstrated $\approx0.7$, eliminates truthful bidding
entirely (truthful rate $0.39\to0.00$), and \emph{lowers} the dispersion of
bid/value ratios ($\operatorname{sd}0.124\to0.081$). For the all-engaged strain the
truthful demonstration produces exact Vickrey truth-telling on every decision
(truthful rate $0.83\to1.00$, dispersion $0.076\to0.000$). Demonstration is thus a
stronger control than instruction: it lowers behavioral variance and makes the
mouse follow the prescribed procedure more faithfully.

the leak). The demonstration result suggests the fix is to induce the deficit by
worked CoT rather than instruction alone. Carrying the entire panel over to a

re-running the model battery, we flag it as a deliberate (cost-bearing) extension
rather than fold it in here.

\section{External Validity: A First-Price Stress Test and Cross-Model Portability}
\label{sec:external}

A panel is useful for screening only if it reproduces known mechanism regularities
and if its verdicts are not an artifact of the base model. This section tests both,
and maps the panel's behaviors onto the human-error theories they are meant to
stand in for.

\subsection{First-price stress test}

We move from the second-price environment to a \emph{first-price} sealed-bid
auction, where theory and human data are sharp: with $n=3$ risk-neutral bidders and
uniform values, the symmetric Bayes--Nash equilibrium is to shade to
$b=\frac{n-1}{n}v=\frac{2}{3}v$, and the canonical experimental regularity is
overbidding \emph{relative} to this risk-neutral benchmark \citep{kagellevin1993}.
Note there is no dominant strategy here---only a best response in beliefs---so the
benchmark is BNE, not dominance.

Figure~\ref{fig:fpsb} and Table~\ref{tab:fpsb} report the panel's first-price
behavior. The three reasoning strains track equilibrium: median bid/value ratios
are $0.65$--$0.67$, on the $\frac23$ BNE line, and across all reasoning decisions
the mean ratio is $0.67$ with $38\%$ of bids above the risk-neutral
benchmark---the documented overbidding regularity. The two error mice separate
cleanly and \emph{mechanistically}: the payment-panic mouse bids near zero, while
the second-price-overgeneralizer mouse bids \emph{above its value} (median ratio
$1.15$) because it applies the second-price intuition ``I will not pay my own bid''
to a mechanism where the winner does pay their own bid. In a first-price auction
that error is not harmless---it yields negative surplus---giving a concrete,
mechanism-level account of a winner's-curse pathology.

\subsection{Cross-model portability and the strict 5-mouse panel}

The first-price result raises a prior question: do the deficit \emph{strains}

strict base of Section~\ref{sec:panel}, distinct from the full $27$-cell
$3\times3\times3$ basis. We use this five-strain set (rather than all $27$) wherever
re-running on a second model would otherwise be prohibitive: here for portability,
and in Section~\ref{sec:ea} as the evolutionary search's evaluator. The five are

\section{Evolutionary Search over Auction Mechanisms}
\label{sec:ea}

So far the panel has \emph{verified} mechanisms picked by hand. The natural next
question is whether the same panel can \emph{drive} a search over mechanisms. This
section makes that loop concrete: why a search is the right tool, what the design
space is, how the operators act on an actual auction, and what the search returns.

\subsection{Why search, and why language models make it possible}

Mechanism design is at bottom a search problem: among the auctions one could
implement, which perform well? The standard answer is analytic---Myerson's optimal
auction, Vickrey--Clarke--Groves, obvious-strategy-proof variants
\citep{li2017,vickrey1961}---each derived in closed form from a tight model of
rational bidders. Replace the rational benchmark with a panel of boundedly rational
digital mice and the closed-form route closes: ``best for the strict $5$-mouse
panel'' is not a function one can differentiate.

An \emph{evolutionary algorithm} is a heuristic search for exactly this situation;
it needs only the ability to \emph{evaluate} a candidate. The loop: (i) hold a
population of $N$ candidate mechanisms; (ii) score each by a fitness $F$; (iii)
retain the top performers (\emph{elites}); (iv) form a new generation by perturbing
elites (\emph{mutation}) and recombining pairs (\emph{crossover}); (v) iterate.
Such searches are routine in machine learning but rare in mechanism design, where
candidates have had to be numeric vectors in a fixed family and bidders simulated
by a closed-form behavioral model. Frontier language models loosen both
constraints: on the bidder side the digital-mouse panel supplies instructable,
cognitively varied agents; on the mechanism side an LLM compiles a structured
\emph{genotype} (a JSON record of payment rule, bid language, reserve, framing)
into a natural-language rule the bidders can act on. Mutation and crossover act on
the genotype---``flip \texttt{bid\_language} from \texttt{continuous} to
\texttt{discrete\_grid}'' is an unambiguous, schema-validated edit---while the
bidder-facing artifact stays language.

\paragraph{What ``fitness'' means here.} $F_{\mathrm{robust}}$~\eqref{eq:frobust}
is panel-based: a candidate scores well only if it is efficient, induces its
declared intended action, and does so \emph{across} the panel, not just for a
rational outlier. The search is therefore explicitly looking for mechanisms that
are robust to bounded rationality, not for mechanisms that are optimal against a
rational benchmark.

\subsection{The operators on a concrete auction}

It helps to watch the operators act on a real mechanism. Take the rank-2 elite, a
\emph{first-price continuous} sealed-bid auction (the gen-$0$ seed). An
\emph{elite} step simply copies it unchanged into the next generation because it is
in the top quartile. A \emph{mutation} step resamples one field---say it flips
\texttt{bid\_language} from \texttt{continuous} to \texttt{discrete\_grid}---and
asks the LLM to rewrite ``submit any bid in \$0.1 increments'' into ``choose a bid
from the grid $\{0,1,5,10,20,30,50\}$,'' producing the rank-3 \emph{first-price
discrete-grid} mechanism. A \emph{crossover} step takes two parents---a first-price
continuous auction and a mechanism carrying a \texttt{decision-table} framing---and,
field by field, draws each child field from one parent, yielding for instance a
first-price continuous auction that inherits the other parent's framing scaffold;
the LLM then rewrites a single coherent rule. A schema validator rejects ill-typed
offspring and duplicates (by rule-text hash) are discarded. The gen-$1$ crossover
child built this way is the eventual rank-1 mechanism.

\subsection{Scale and result}

The run uses $24$ individuals for $2$ generations against the strict $5$-mouse
panel with $2$ repetitions per mouse---$48$ mechanism evaluations and $480$ raw
bidder-decisions, on \texttt{gpt-5.4-mini}. It is a proof-of-concept of the
EA-plus-panel loop, not a paper-scale search. Figure~\ref{fig:ea} and
Table~\ref{tab:ea} report two findings. First, the search \emph{improves}: the
best-of-generation fitness rises from $3.37$ (a gen-$0$ initialization) to $3.42$ (a
gen-$1$ crossover child), which remains elite through gen-$2$. Second, and more
substantively, the top of the population concentrates on canonical sealed-bid
designs: three of the top five evolved mechanisms are first-price sealed-bid
(continuous or discrete-grid), one is second-price continuous, and one is a
posted-price binary-accept design; all five share no reserve and random
tie-breaking. The EA, given a permissive genotype space and the digital-mouse panel
as evaluator, elevates first-price continuous---exactly the mechanism the a-priori
stress test of Section~\ref{sec:external} picked---to the top.

\subsection{A transparent re-ranking by SMAD}

$F_{\mathrm{robust}}$ is a multi-objective composite and hence hard to read. As a
check we re-rank the evolved mechanisms by SMAD~\eqref{eq:smad}, the percentage
deviation of realized bids from each mechanism's \emph{own} declared optimum
(Figure~\ref{fig:smad}). The two rankings broadly agree---the Spearman correlation
between $-\mathrm{SMAD}$ and $F_{\mathrm{robust}}$ is $0.54$---but they tell
complementary stories, and the disagreement is informative. Second-price mechanisms
attain the \emph{lowest} SMAD (the best second-price design reaches
$\mathrm{SMAD}\approx37$, and second-price continuous $\approx39$): under a
strategy-proof rule, bidders find the mechanism's optimum (truthful bidding) most
easily. First-price mechanisms---which \emph{win} on $F_{\mathrm{robust}}$---sit
higher ($\approx45$--$51$), and third-price is worst ($\approx66$). The reading is
clean: second-price is the easiest mechanism to bid in \emph{optimally}, while
first-price is the mechanism the panel-based robustness objective \emph{prefers}.
Both statements are true and they answer different questions, which is exactly why
we report both rather than collapsing to one number.

We read the overall agreement as \emph{convergent validation} of two
procedures---the a-priori choice of Section~\ref{sec:external} rests on sharp theory
and human data; the EA choice rests on panel-based robustness; they agree, which
they need not have---while the SMAD/fitness split is a reminder of the search's
actual objective. The search is explicitly looking for mechanisms that are robust to
bounded rationality, not for mechanisms that minimize bidders' deviation from a
rational optimum; first-price wins the former and second-price the latter.

\paragraph{Caveats.} Two generations is a proof-of-concept, not a paper-scale
search; the convergence is qualitative agreement with Section~\ref{sec:external},
not a statement about optimality over the full genotype space. The weights are the
calibration-aligned defaults of Section~\ref{sec:fitness} and have not been re-tuned
to this search. Cross-model re-scoring of survivors, along the lines of the
strict-panel portability check, would flag candidates that score well on the search
model but degrade on a stronger held-out model.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Procedural basis versus authored error mice.} Construct validity is
strongest for the procedural basis, where a single instruction (engage an axis)
produces the predicted directional movement. Calibration is more nuanced: the basis
covers truthful and underbidding behavior on its own, but overbidding---a
first-order regularity in human data \citep{kagellevin1993}---is recovered only with
hand-specified heuristic strains. The model-agnostic claim applies cleanly to the
procedural axes; the error mice are a complementary, more bespoke device for
spanning the mistake space, and the two should be reported as distinct epistemic
objects.

\paragraph{Digital twins as funhouse mirrors.} Our design is a direct response to
the distortions \citet{peng2025funhouse} document in LLM digital twins. We do not
attempt the thing those distortions make unreliable---reproducing the full
distribution of human opinions or behaviors with a single model---because that is
both unattainable and heavily model-dependent. Instead we exploit one distortion,
hyper-rationality, as a clean substrate to engineer \emph{specific} deficits from,
and we measure model-dependence directly rather than assuming it away
(Section~\ref{sec:external}). The reasoning audit (Section~\ref{sec:audit}) is in
the same spirit: it is an internal-validity check that the induced deficit is the
one we intended, not an emergent artifact.

\paragraph{Model dependence.} Because deficits are procedural controls, the protocol
can be rerun on a different frontier model. At the strain level, four of five
reserved strict strains preserve their dominant class across base models; the lone
exception is the contingent-reasoning deficit, which a stronger model tends to
repair into truthful bidding. This is a feature, not a bug---the panel is the
instrument that makes such repair visible---but it cautions against reading
single-model panel scores as model-invariant and identifies contingent reasoning as
the deficit most in need of a hardened scaffold; the demonstration result
(Section~\ref{sec:cot}) is one remedy.

\paragraph{External validity.} Digital mice are a phase-I screening population. A
strain reproducing a mistake class establishes that the panel can express that
behavior, not that the human share of that behavior is matched. We therefore keep
the stress-testing claim (this paper) separate from any calibrated prediction of
human behavior, which would require human pilot data---the subject of the next
section.

\section{Toward Validation with Human Experiments}
\label{sec:validation}

The pipeline so far ends in a short list of candidate mechanisms; it does not end
in a human-validated one. The final link is to test the panel's verdicts against
people, and the integrative-experiment framing makes the test well-defined rather
than ad hoc \citep{almaatouq2024,alsobay2025integrative}. The design space of
Table~\ref{tab:designspace} is exactly the pre-committed family over which one can
make externally valid statements: draw a random sample of mechanisms from it,
assign them to human bidders, and compare two things. First, \emph{behavioral}
validation---do human bidders in a sampled mechanism produce the bid distribution
the digital-mouse panel predicted for it (truthful core plus the documented
overbidding tail in first-price, etc.)? Second, \emph{ranking} validation---does the
panel's robustness ordering over mechanisms (first-price near the top) survive
contact with human subjects, the way the simulated open-auction clearing price in
related work matched both theory and field data \citep{manning2024automated}?

This is also where the limits become testable rather than rhetorical. The error
mice are calibrated to \emph{support}, not \emph{share}; human pilot data would turn
the calibration scorecard from a coverage claim into a moment-matching one, letting
us weight the panel so that the share of overbidders matches the human share in a
given population. And because the genotype space is pre-committed, even mechanisms
that happen to appear in a model's training data are handled by the random-sampling
logic, which only reduces prediction error. We leave the human experiments
themselves to future work; the contribution here is the screening instrument and the
evidence that it is internally valid, externally discriminating, and ready to be
pointed at people.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We show that a frontier reasoning model can be instructed into stable, separable
cognitive deficits whose behavior in a second-price sealed-bid auction (i) moves in
the theory-predicted direction as each cognitive axis is engaged, (ii) spans the
canonical space of human SPSB mistakes once a small set of targeted error strains is
added, and (iii) is internally faithful---an audit of the models' own reasoning
shows the induced deficit is the intended one, though leakily so under the standard
base. In a first-price stress test the reasoning strains shade to the risk-neutral
BNE and overbid relative to it while an overgeneralizing strain bids above value and
loses money, four of five reserved deficits preserve their class across base models,
and an evolutionary search driven by the panel recovers first-price sealed-bid as
the top of the population---agreeing with the a-priori choice, even as a transparent
SMAD re-ranking shows second-price is the easiest mechanism to bid in optimally.
Together these support a research program in which instructed language-model agents
are used as a phase-I population to screen mechanisms for robustness to bounded
rationality \emph{before} human-subject experiments are run---not as a replacement
for those experiments, but as the instrument that decides which mechanisms are worth
running. The immediate next steps are to migrate the panel to a strict, CoT-induced
construction, to extend the evolutionary and cross-model analyses at scale, and to
carry both protocols into a second domain (matching) and into the human-subject
validation of Section~\ref{sec:validation}.

\section{Prompts}
\label{app:prompts}

\paragraph{Standard base.} ``Your TOP PRIORITY is to place bids which maximize your
profit in the long run. Learn from the history of previous rounds in order to
maximize your total profit. Don't forget the values are redrawn independently each
round.''

\paragraph{Strict base.} ``You are acting as a digital mouse with a specified
cognitive procedure. Your TOP PRIORITY is to follow the cognitive procedure exactly.
Do not repair, override, or improve the procedure even if you know a more rational
auction strategy. If the cognitive procedure conflicts with the profit-maximizing
strategy, the cognitive procedure wins. In your plan, use only the reasoning
operations explicitly allowed below. Do not invoke auction theorems,
dominant-strategy labels, or Vickrey truth-telling shortcuts unless the allowed
procedure below gives you enough reasoning steps to derive them.''

\paragraph{Cognitive axes (level 0 / level 2).} \emph{Contingent reasoning.}
(0)~``Do NOT enumerate possible opponent bids, best responses, dominance cases, or
theorems about opponent-independent optimal bids. Treat the auction as a fixed
environment and choose from a simple own-value rule.'' (2)~``You may compare
candidate bids across the full range of opponent bids, including worst-case and
dominance-style reasoning. If one bid is robust across all cases, use it.''
\emph{Forward planning.} (0)~``Do NOT trace through what happens after different
candidate bids. Do NOT compare expected profits. Use a margin-of-safety heuristic:
bid below your value\,\dots\ for values above 5, choose roughly 60--80 percent of
your value; for very low values, bid zero or a tiny amount.'' (2)~``Build a decision
tree over several candidate bids. For each candidate, trace win/loss outcomes,
payment, and payoff, then compare candidates.'' \emph{Beliefs about others.}
(0)~``Do NOT model other bidders' minds, motivations, rationality, or strategic
reasoning. Do not assume they will bid truthfully or optimize. Treat them as an
unknown background.'' (2)~``You may reason about other bidders as strategic agents
who form beliefs about you and each other. Use second-order strategic reasoning when
choosing a bid.'' Level-1 paragraphs are intermediate (enumerate a few cases /
one-step lookahead / first-order beliefs).

\paragraph{Error mice.} \emph{Second-price overgeneralizer:} ``You strongly focus on
the fact that the winner pays the second-highest bid, not their own bid.
Overgeneralize this rule: treat bidding above your value as mostly safe because you
expect not to pay your own bid.'' \emph{Payment panic:} ``You are afraid that your
bid is the amount you might have to pay\,\dots\ choose the smallest positive bid
allowed by the auction grid\,\dots\ Even if your value is high, protect yourself by
bidding 0.1 rather than trying to win.''

\paragraph{CoT demonstration (Section~\ref{sec:cot}).} A single worked example is
appended after the cognitive-style block and before the live decision. It fixes a
sample value, walks through only the allowed reasoning, and ends in a bid: a
margin-of-safety underbid ($\approx0.7\times$ value) for the all-ablated strain, and
the contingent/forward/belief reasoning that yields a truthful Vickrey bid for the
all-engaged strain.

\section{A Planned Outcome-Instruction Robustness Check}
\label{app:direct}

The panel induces deficits by constraining the reasoning \emph{procedure}. A
complementary design, which we specify here and leave to a (cost-bearing) re-run,
induces them by naming the \emph{outcome} directly: rather than telling a mouse
\emph{how} to think, tell it \emph{what} it does---``you tend to overbid,'' ``you
tend to underbid,'' ``you bid your value.'' Comparing outcome-instructed strains to
the procedure-instructed strains of this paper would separate two explanations for
the observed signatures: genuine procedural induction versus simple compliance with
a labeled behavior. The prediction is that procedure-instructed strains should
match their outcome-instructed counterparts on the targeted metric but differ on
\emph{off-target} behavior and on the reasoning audit of Section~\ref{sec:audit}
(an outcome instruction need not, and likely will not, change the dominance-argument
invocation rate). We flag this as the cleanest single robustness experiment to run
next.
